Coordinate Reference Systems

Session 4 · Where is this data, really?

Why this session

In your homework, two layers that should have overlapped didn’t. Something failed loudly, something failed silently, or both. Today is about why, so that you can spot the problem in any data and in any code an AI writes for you.

By the end of this session you should be able to:

  1. Tell a geographic CRS from a projected one by reading R’s printout.
  2. Explain what a datum is and when a datum difference matters.
  3. Explain why every flat map distorts something, and choose a projection that protects what your analysis measures.
  4. Use EPSG codes to name, check, and convert CRSs in R.

2 Geographic vs. projected CRSs

A coordinate reference system (CRS) is the set of rules that turns a pair of numbers into a place on Earth. There are two kinds.

  • A geographic CRS gives positions as angles on a model of the Earth: longitude and latitude, in degrees. EPSG:4326 (WGS 84) is the most common one.
  • A projected CRS flattens that model onto a plane and gives positions as x and y distances, usually in meters (sometimes feet). Every projected CRS is built on top of a geographic one.

The key consequence: a degree is not a fixed distance. A degree of latitude is always about 111 km. A degree of longitude shrinks as you move toward the poles, because the meridians converge.

So a “square” one degree on a side is not square on the ground, and its area depends on where it is. That is why lengths, areas, and buffers measured directly in degrees are meaningless.

NoteA helpful shortcut in sf

When you ask sf for an area or distance on longitude/latitude data, it does the math on the globe and gives you meters (st_area(cantons) works). Don’t count on every function being this careful. A raster’s cell size, for example, stays in degrees.

3 Datums and ellipsoids

Latitude and longitude only mean something once you say which model of the Earth they’re measured on.

  • The Earth is close to an ellipsoid: a sphere squashed at the poles. The equatorial radius is about 21 km longer than the polar radius.
  • An ellipsoid is the mathematical shape (its size and flattening).
  • A datum is an ellipsoid plus how it is pinned to the real Earth: where its center is and how it’s oriented.

Change the datum and the same coordinates point to a slightly different spot on the ground.

Datum Ellipsoid Pinned to Where you’ll meet it
NAD27 Clarke 1866 A survey point in Kansas (Meades Ranch) Old maps and surveys
NAD83 GRS 80 Earth’s center (fixed to the North American plate) US Census / TIGER data, state agencies
WGS 84 WGS 84 Earth’s center (global) GPS, most web data, EPA TRI

Let’s measure how much a datum matters. Take one set of coordinates for the AU campus and label it with two datums:

# One pair of numbers: longitude, latitude
au_xy <- st_point(c(-77.0887, 38.9365))

# The same numbers, labeled with two different datums
au_nad27 <- st_sfc(au_xy, crs = 4267)
au_nad83 <- st_sfc(au_xy, crs = 4269)

# Convert the NAD27 point to NAD83, so both are on one datum
au_nad27_converted <- st_transform(au_nad27, 4269)

# How far apart are "the same" coordinates on the ground?
st_distance(au_nad83, au_nad27_converted)
Units: [m]
         [,1]
[1,] 28.71194

About 28 meters, which is enough to put a facility on the wrong side of a street or a census tract boundary. Between NAD83 and WGS 84, the difference in the US is only about a meter or two. That rarely matters for tract-level analysis or 30 m rasters.

But R can’t know what precision your question needs, so it treats any difference in CRS as a mismatch. That’s why, in the asthma problem, the EPA TRI points (WGS 84) still have to be transformed before they can be joined to the Census tracts (NAD83).

NoteBehind the scenes: datum shifts need extra files

Accurate NAD27 → NAD83 conversion uses a grid of measured shifts. That grid is a separate download, not built into R. If PROJ (the library under sf and terra) can’t find it, it falls back to a “ballpark” conversion. That can mean no shift at all, with no error. This page was built with sf_proj_network(TRUE), which lets PROJ fetch grids from the internet as needed. You’ll rarely need NAD27 in this course. The lesson is the general one: a step that runs without error isn’t proof that it did what you think.

4 Projections: flattening is a trade

You can’t flatten an orange peel without stretching or tearing it. Map projections are systematic ways of doing that stretching. They come in families, named after the flat surface the globe is projected onto:

  • Cylindrical: wrap a cylinder around the globe (Mercator, UTM).
  • Conic: set a cone on top of it (Albers, Lambert Conformal Conic). Good for mid-latitude regions that are wider than they are tall, like the US or Maryland.
  • Azimuthal: touch a flat plane to one point (polar maps, “distance from here” maps).

Each projection protects some properties and gives up others:

  • Conformal: preserves shape (angles) locally. Areas get distorted.
  • Equal-area: preserves area. Shapes get distorted.
  • Equidistant: preserves distance, but only from one point or along certain lines.
  • Azimuthal: preserves direction from the center point.

No flat map preserves all of these. In particular, a map can’t be both conformal and equal-area. So you choose a projection by asking what your analysis measures.

How big is Greenland, really?

On Mercator, Greenland looks about as big as Africa. Drag it south and watch what happens on each map. Its true size never changes. The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC, in blue) stays put for comparison.

viewof targetLat = Inputs.range([0, 73], {
  value: 73, step: 1, label: "Greenland's center latitude (° N)"
})
function moveTo(feature, newLat) {
  const [lon0, lat0] = d3.geoCentroid(feature);
  const toOrigin = d3.geoRotation([-lon0, -lat0]);   // centroid -> (0, 0)
  const toTarget = d3.geoRotation([-lon0, -newLat]); // (lon0, newLat) -> (0, 0)
  const move = pt => toTarget.invert(toOrigin(pt));
  const walk = c => typeof c[0] === "number" ? move(c) : c.map(walk);
  return {type: "Feature", geometry: {
    type: feature.geometry.type, coordinates: walk(feature.geometry.coordinates)
  }};
}

greenland = countries.find(d => d.properties.name === "Greenland")
drc = countries.find(d => d.properties.name === "Dem. Rep. Congo")
movedGreenland = moveTo(greenland, targetLat)

{
  const wide = width > 700;
  const w = wide ? Math.floor((width - 40) / 2) : width;
  const h = Math.round(w * 0.75);

  // Draw one panel and report Greenland's apparent size relative to the DRC
  function panel(title, makeProjection, view) {
    const projection = makeProjection().fitExtent([[6, 6], [w - 6, h - 6]], fitTarget(view));
    const path = d3.geoPath(projection);
    const svg = d3.create("svg").attr("viewBox", [0, 0, w, h]).style("width", "100%");
    svg.append("path").attr("d", path({type: "Sphere"})).attr("fill", "#eef3f7");
    svg.append("path").attr("d", path(land)).attr("fill", "#dcd8cf");
    svg.append("path").attr("d", path(greenland))
      .attr("fill", "none").attr("stroke", "#d1495b").attr("stroke-dasharray", "3 3");
    svg.append("path").attr("d", path(drc)).attr("fill", "#1f5f8b");
    svg.append("path").attr("d", path(movedGreenland)).attr("fill", "#d1495b");
    const looks = path.area(movedGreenland) / path.area(drc);
    return html`<div>
      <div style="font-weight:600">${title}</div>${svg.node()}
      <div>Greenland looks <span class="readout">${looks.toFixed(1)}×</span> the DRC</div>
    </div>`;
  }

  // True ratio, measured on the globe
  const truth = d3.geoArea(greenland) / d3.geoArea(drc);

  return html`<div class="widget">
    <div style="display:${wide ? "grid" : "block"};grid-template-columns:1fr 1fr;gap:1rem">
      ${panel("Mercator (conformal)", d3.geoMercator, "merc")}
      ${panel("Equal Earth (equal-area)", d3.geoEqualEarth, "world")}
    </div>
    <p style="margin:.75rem 0 0">On the globe, Greenland is
      <b>${truth.toFixed(2)}×</b> the size of the DRC, wherever you put it.
      The dashed outline is where Greenland actually is.</p>
  </div>`;
}

5 EPSG codes: names for CRSs

Saying “NAD83, Lambert Conformal Conic, standard parallels 38.3° and 39.45°, meters” every time would be painful. The EPSG registry (now maintained by IOGP, the oil and gas producers’ association) gives each complete CRS definition a number. One code bundles the datum, the ellipsoid, the projection and its parameters, and the units.

Codes you’ll meet in this course:

EPSG Name Type Units Where you’ll see it
4326 WGS 84 Geographic degrees GPS, most web data, EPA TRI
4269 NAD83 Geographic degrees Census tracts via tidycensus
4267 NAD27 Geographic degrees Old maps and surveys
3857 WGS 84 / Pseudo-Mercator Projected, cylindrical “meters” (stretched) Web map tiles. Display only.
5070 NAD83 / Conus Albers Projected, conic, equal-area meters NLCD; areas across the US
26918 NAD83 / UTM zone 18N Projected, transverse Mercator meters Distances in DC, MD, VA
26985 NAD83 / Maryland Projected, Lambert conic meters Maryland state data
2248 NAD83 / Maryland (ftUS) Projected, Lambert conic US feet Maryland county GIS. Check the units!

6 Back to the failure: fix it and check it

Now the symptoms from section 1 make sense:

  • The cantons’ coordinates are around 6 and 50 (degrees). The raster’s are in the tens of thousands (meters).
  • plot() just draws numbers. It put the cantons in a tiny patch near x = 6, y = 50, far outside the raster’s extent, so nothing appeared on the map.
  • crop() compared the two extents, found no overlap, and stopped.
  • extract() found no raster cells inside any polygon and returned NaN, without complaint.

The fix is to put both layers in one CRS. Transform the vector to match the raster, not the other way around. Moving polygon vertices is exact, while reprojecting a raster has to resample cell values.

WarningLabeling is not converting

st_transform() converts coordinates: the numbers change. st_set_crs() (or st_crs(x) <- ...) only relabels them: the numbers stay the same. Relabeling is correct only when a file has no CRS and you know what it should be. AI assistants sometimes suggest it as a “fix” for a mismatch. Here’s what that does:

# Wrong: claims the degrees are now meters
cantons_relabeled <- st_set_crs(cantons, 2169)
Warning: st_crs<- : replacing crs does not reproject data; use st_transform for that
st_bbox(cantons_relabeled)
     xmin      ymin      xmax      ymax

R warns you, and the bounding box gives it away: the “meters” are still 5.7 to 50.2. In EPSG:2169, coordinates that small sit at the grid’s origin, which is in France, about 120 km southwest of Luxembourg’s center.

The habit that ties it together: check the CRS before any operation that combines two layers, and check the result after. Most CRS mistakes don’t produce errors. They produce wrong answers.

8 Check yourself

Note1. A bounding box reads xmin: 316268 ymin: 4295630 xmax: 334407 ymax: 4318264. Degrees or meters? Projected or geographic?

Meters, so it’s projected. Longitudes only go to ±180 and latitudes to ±90. Numbers in the hundreds of thousands and millions look like UTM. (This is Washington, DC, in UTM zone 18N.)

Note2. Why did extract() return NaN instead of an error?

It did what it was asked: it looked for raster cells inside each polygon and found none, because the polygons sat far outside the raster’s extent. An empty result isn’t an error to the function. Only you know it’s wrong, which is why you check for NAs after every extract.

Note3. An AI’s code calls st_transform(tracts, 3857) before computing st_area(). What’s the problem?

Web Mercator inflates area by a factor that grows with latitude. At Maryland’s latitude (about 39° N), that’s about 1.66×, so every area would be about two-thirds too large. Use 5070 or 26985, or compute the area on the original lon/lat data.

Note4. TRI facilities are in EPSG:4326 and tracts are in EPSG:4269. The difference is about a meter. Why transform anyway?

Because sf requires both layers to have the same CRS before it will join them, and it can’t judge whether a meter matters for your question. Transform one to match the other (or both to a projected CRS you’ll measure in) and the join runs.
